HISTORY AND LEGACY

HOW THE NES CHANGED GAMING

1983 — Famicom launches in Japan.
Nintendo releases the Family Computer, or Famicom. It uses cartridges and features a distinctive red-and-white design.

1985 — North American launch.
Nintendo introduces the redesigned NES in North America. Its arrival comes after the major North American video game market crash of 1983.

1986 — More major franchises arrive.
The Legend of Zelda and Metroid help establish new styles of exploration and adventure.

1988 — Mega Man 2.
Capcom releases one of the system's best-known action platformers.

Legacy.
The NES helped establish Nintendo as a major force in the video game industry. Its franchises, characters, and design ideas continue to influence games today.

HARDWARE DEEP DIVE

Picture: The NES outputs a 256 × 240 pixel picture, using a dedicated picture-processing chip.

Sound: Its audio hardware supports several different sound channels, helping create the distinctive 8-bit music associated with the system.

Cartridges: NES cartridges could contain extra hardware, including memory-mapping chips. These helped developers create larger and more complex games.

Lockout system: North American NES systems used the 10NES security chip to help restrict unauthorized cartridges.

Accessories: The system supported accessories such as the NES Zapper light gun and the R.O.B. accessory.

Nintendo's legendary 16-bit console • 1990–2003

The 16-bit revolution.

The Super Nintendo Entertainment System, also known as the SNES, was Nintendo's successor to the NES. It brought improved graphics, sound, and gameplay to the home console market.

Technology that mattered

Mode 7

A graphics mode capable of rotating and scaling a background layer. It helped create effects like the racing tracks in Super Mario Kart.

Super FX

An additional cartridge chip that helped produce advanced graphics, including 3D effects.

Enhancement chips

Certain cartridges included extra hardware to expand the console's capabilities.

NINTENDO 64

1996 · Nintendo · Fifth generation

The 64-bit era

Nintendo's leap into 3D gaming. The Nintendo 64 brought groundbreaking 3D adventures, multiplayer experiences, and some of gaming's most recognizable titles.

Console overview

The Nintendo 64 launched in Japan in June 1996 and North America in September 1996. It was Nintendo's successor to the Super Nintendo Entertainment System.

Unlike many competitors that moved to CD-based games, Nintendo continued using cartridges. The console became famous for its 3D platformers, local multiplayer, and distinctive controller.

Hardware specifications

Architecture 64-bit

Main memory 4 MB RDRAM
Expansion Pak Adds another 4 MB

Generation Fifth generation
Common resolution 320 × 240
Release year 1996

The controller

The Nintendo 64 controller featured a unique three-pronged design. Its central analog stick helped make precise 3D movement possible on a home console.

It also featured the iconic A and B buttons, a D-pad, shoulder buttons, a Z trigger, and a C-button cluster.

The controller helped establish analog sticks as an important part of 3D console gaming.

Super Mario 64

A landmark 3D platformer.

A memorable space shooter.

Donkey Kong 64

A huge 3D collectathon.

What made it special?

  • Analog stick controls for 3D movement.
  • Four controller ports built into the console.
  • Cartridge-based games with fast loading.
  • The Expansion Pak upgraded available memory.
  • Innovative 3D worlds and camera systems.
  • Rumble Pak support for compatible games.

Sales and legacy

32.93 million

The Nintendo 64 helped shape the future of 3D gaming. Its influence can be seen in platformers, adventure games, multiplayer titles, and controller design.

NINTENDO GAMECUBE

Nintendo's sixth-generation console • 2001

Overview

The Nintendo GameCube was released in 2001 in Japan and North America. It was Nintendo's successor to the Nintendo 64 and competed with the PlayStation 2 and original Xbox. Its compact design, distinctive controller, and mini optical discs made it one of Nintendo's most recognizable consoles.

The controller

The GameCube controller featured:
  • Two analog sticks
  • A large A button
  • B, X and Y face buttons
  • Pressure-sensitive L and R triggers
  • A Z shoulder button
  • Built-in rumble support
Its unusual button layout became iconic and remained popular with competitive Super Smash Bros. players.

Special features

  • Four built-in controller ports.
  • Memory cards for saving games.
  • Game Boy Player accessory for Game Boy games.
  • Broadband and modem adapters for selected online features.
  • Link connectivity with the Game Boy Advance.
  • Digital AV output on certain early hardware revisions.
